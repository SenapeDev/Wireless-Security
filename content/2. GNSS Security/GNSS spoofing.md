## Introduction to GNSS jamming
Spoofing is a fundamentally different and more dangerous class of attack than [[GNSS jamming|jamming]]. Rather than overwhelming the receiver with noise, a spoofer transmits **counterfeit GNSS-like signals** that are designed to be accepted by the victim receiver as authentic satellite transmissions. The objective is to cause the receiver to compute a false position, velocity, or time solution without the user being aware that anything is wrong.

For a spoofing attack to succeed without triggering alarms, the counterfeit signals must be as consistent as possible with what a genuine receiver would observe. An ideal spoofer must match the signals from all satellites expected to be visible from the victim's location, be synchronized to the GNSS time scale, provide a consistent navigation message, replicate the correct direction of arrival for each satellite, and match realistic power levels and Doppler shifts corresponding to the satellite geometry and receiver motion. Meeting all of these requirements simultaneously is technically demanding and expensive, which is why spoofing attacks vary widely in sophistication and cost.

## Types of spoofing attacks

### Meaconing
**Meaconing** is the simplest form of spoofing-class attack. It involves receiving a block of GNSS radio frequency spectrum and rebroadcasting it, typically with added delay and higher power to override the genuine signals at the victim receiver. Crucially, meaconing does not distinguish between individual satellite signals: the entire received spectrum is retransmitted as a block. The delay introduced by the meaconer is common to all satellite signals in the block. From the victim receiver's perspective, all pseudoranges are shifted by the same amount, which is geometrically equivalent to observing all satellites from the meaconer's position (at the time of reception) rather than from the victim's true position. Because the delay is common to all channels, it cannot be separated from the receiver clock bias in the standard PVT estimation, and the receiver will compute a position consistent with the meaconer's location rather than the victim's true location. Meaconing is relatively easy to implement in hardware, requiring only a GNSS antenna, a low-noise amplifier, and an RF retransmitting front-end, optionally combined with a recording and playback system to introduce variable delays. Detection is possible if the introduced delay is inconsistent with the receiver's internal clock drift model, but it is very difficult for near-zero delays. At the signal level, time-dependent encryption of the navigation message would increase robustness against meaconing.

### Simplistic spoofing
**Simplistic spoofing** involves generating counterfeit GNSS signals from scratch, typically using a commercial GNSS signal simulator, without synchronizing them to the real signals currently being broadcast by the satellites. The fake signals look like noise to a receiver that is already tracking real signals (because they are not synchronized with the genuine spreading codes), but they can cause the receiver to lose lock and undergo a re-acquisition process, during which it may latch onto the fake signals instead of the real ones. This leads to a discontinuous jump in the reported position, which is a significant artifact that makes simplistic spoofing relatively easy to detect. The attack is straightforward to implement with commercial hardware but has limited effectiveness against receivers that monitor for sudden position discontinuities.

### Intermediate spoofing
**Intermediate spoofing** is a more sophisticated approach in which the fake signals are carefully synchronized with the real GNSS signals, matching the spreading code phase of each satellite's genuine signal. The spoofer first observes the real signals to determine the code phase alignment, then gradually increases the power of the counterfeit signals until they overtake the genuine ones and the receiver's tracking loops lock onto the fake signals. By smoothly shifting the code phase of the counterfeit signals after taking control, the attacker can steer the receiver's position estimate continuously and gradually to a desired false location without causing noticeable jumps. This attack requires knowledge of the target receiver's position and velocity, and the hardware consists of a modified GNSS receiver combined with an RF transmitting front-end. Intermediate spoofing is difficult to detect with simple methods and requires complex countermeasures such as angle-of-arrival monitoring.

### Sophisticated spoofing
**Sophisticated spoofing** represents the most capable attack category. It employs a network of multiple coordinated spoofers that replicate not only the content of the satellite signals but also their spatial characteristics, including the direction of arrival and the mutual phase relationships between signals as seen from the victim's antenna. This allows the attack to defeat even multi-antenna countermeasures that rely on spatial signal properties. Sophisticated spoofing requires sub-centimeter knowledge of the target receiver's antenna position and velocity, multiple phase-locked portable transmitters, and significant coordination. It is the most effective spoofing category but also the most complex and expensive to mount, and it is very rare in practice.

### Cyberspoofing
**Cyberspoofing** operates at a completely different level from the RF attacks described above. Rather than transmitting fake radio signals, cyberspoofing attacks the software or communication layer of the device. A cyberattacker intercepts or replaces the location data reported by the GNSS receiver to the application or service using it, without interfering with the RF signals at all. This is essentially a man-in-the-middle attack on the location data channel. The attack can be launched remotely and requires no physical proximity to the target. Implementation vectors include malicious software updates, root-level system compromise, injection of falsified digital maps, manipulation of GNSS reference stations, or theft of cryptographic signing credentials. A well-known benign example of cyberspoofing is the use of location spoofing apps on smartphones to falsify one's GPS coordinates in location-based games; however, the same technique applied to safety-critical or financial systems represents a serious threat.


## Spoofing detection

Detecting spoofing requires monitoring signal observables that reveal the anomalous nature of the counterfeit signals. The most important approach is **cross-checking the C/N0 and AGC** measurements, which respond in characteristic and opposite ways to jamming versus spoofing.

Under a **spoofing attack**, the spoofer injects a strong, signal-like counterfeit transmission. The counterfeit signal appears to the receiver as a legitimate satellite signal rather than noise, so the numerator of the C/N0 estimate increases. The reported C/N0 rises across the affected channels. However, the total RF power received is also higher than normal because of the added spoofer signal, so the AGC still reduces its gain. The distinguishing signature of spoofing is therefore a simultaneous **increase in C/N0 and decrease in AGC gain**, in contrast to jamming where both decrease together.

This cross-check between C/N0 and AGC is the foundation of the spoofing detection logic implemented in tools such as Google's GnssLogger application for Android, which monitors both parameters in real time and compares the last 10 observations to the previous 50. A possible jamming event is flagged when C/N0 and AGC both drop by more than 20% simultaneously. A possible spoofing event is flagged when C/N0 increases while AGC decreases simultaneously, or when the difference between the GNSS-derived time and the internet NTP time exceeds one second (which would indicate that the receiver's time has been manipulated by the spoofer).

## Anti-spoofing techniques

Anti-spoofing countermeasures fall into two broad categories: **non-cryptographic** and **cryptographic** defenses.

- Non-cryptographic defenses operate at the receiver level and do not require any changes to the satellite signal structure. Several approaches exist. **Stand-alone signal processing** methods look for inconsistencies in the observables computed from the received signals, such as anomalies in pseudorange residuals, implausible pseudorange rates, or sudden jumps in the navigation message content. 
	- **Authentication via time consistency** cross-checks the timing information derived from GNSS against independent time references to detect delays introduced by meaconing or signal replay attacks. 
	- **Integration with other sensors** uses inertial measurement units (IMUs), barometers, odometers, or other independent sensors to verify that the GNSS-derived position and velocity are physically consistent with what the other sensors indicate; a spoofer that steers the reported position to a location inconsistent with inertial measurements will trigger an alarm. 
	- **Antenna-aided techniques**, specifically *direction-of-arrival* (**DOA**) monitoring using multi-element antenna arrays, exploit the fact that each satellite signal should arrive from a specific direction in the sky; if a spoofer transmits all counterfeit signals from a single ground-level antenna, their apparent direction of arrival will not match the expected sky geometry, exposing the attack.

- Cryptographic defenses address the fundamental vulnerability that GNSS signals were originally designed without authentication mechanisms. Since the navigation message is transmitted in plaintext without any cryptographic protection, anyone with the appropriate hardware can forge a signal carrying any desired navigation data. Four main cryptographic approaches have been identified.
	- **Navigation Message Authentication (NMA)** protects the navigation data bits by attaching a digital signature or message authentication code (MAC) to the broadcast navigation message while keeping the message itself in plaintext. 
	- **Navigation Message Encryption (NME)** encrypts the entire navigation message, restricting access to authorized receivers. 
	- **Spreading Code Authentication (SCA)** inserts unpredictable, cryptographically protected chips or watermarking sequences into the spreading code, binding authentication to the code-level signal structure; these chips are verified a posteriori using cryptographic functions. 
	- **Spreading Code Encryption (SCE)** encrypts the entire spreading code sequence, making it inaccessible to unauthorized receivers; this is the approach used for restricted signals such as the GPS P(Y) code and the Galileo Public Regulated Service (PRS).

For the open civilian service, the most significant deployed cryptographic countermeasure is **Galileo's Open Service Navigation Message Authentication (OSNMA)**, a free-access service that allows any user to verify that the received Galileo navigation message originates from the Galileo system and has not been tampered with. OSNMA is based on the **TESLA protocol** (Timed Efficient Stream Loss-tolerant Authentication), which is structured around two elements. First, a **Message Authentication Code (MAC)** is computed over the plaintext navigation data and transmitted as part of the signal. Second, the **key** used to compute the MAC is disclosed with a delay in a subsequent transmission. The keys are organized in a **hash chain**: starting from a random secret key $k_N$, each key is derived from the next by applying a one-way hash function, yielding a chain $k_N, k_{N-1}, \ldots, k_1, k_0$, where $k_0$ is the public root key. Because the hash function is one-way, knowing $k_0$ does not allow an attacker to predict future keys in the chain, while the receiver can verify any disclosed key by hashing forward to the root. The root key $k_0$ is digitally signed and certified as authentic by the Galileo ground infrastructure. The delayed disclosure of keys is what gives TESLA its security: at the time the MAC is transmitted, the corresponding key has not yet been revealed, so a spoofer cannot forge a valid MAC. When the key is later disclosed, the receiver can retroactively authenticate the navigation message. An OSNMA-capable receiver must retrieve the dedicated OSNMA fields from the navigation message, implement the required cryptographic functions, synchronize to Galileo System Time (GST) with an accuracy between 30 and 300 seconds before beginning OSNMA processing, and maintain the integrity of stored cryptographic material.
