## Introduction to GNSS interference
[[GNSS introduction|GNSS]] signals are inherently fragile from a radio-frequency perspective. The signals transmitted by satellites orbit at altitudes exceeding 20,000 km, and by the time they reach a ground-level receiver, their power is extraordinarily low. The signal is completely buried in thermal noise: the typical signal-to-noise ratio at the receiver input ranges from -29 dB to -21 dB, meaning the noise power is roughly 100 to 1000 times stronger than the useful signal.

Receivers are able to extract the navigation signal from noise by exploiting the **spread spectrum** nature of GNSS transmissions. Each satellite broadcasts a Direct Sequence Spread Spectrum (DSSS) signal using a known pseudorandom noise (PRN) spreading code. The receiver correlates the incoming signal with a locally generated replica of that code, causing the desired signal to accumulate coherently while uncorrelated noise averages out. This process effectively provides processing gain, making the signal recoverable even when it is well below the noise floor. However, this robustness has a fundamental limit: if an external interfering signal is strong enough relative to the GNSS signal, it can overwhelm the correlator and prevent the receiver from acquiring or tracking the satellite.

This sensitivity to interference is the root cause of all GNSS threat categories. Even a relatively low-power interfering transmitter, if placed sufficiently close to the victim receiver, can render GNSS navigation completely unavailable or, in more sophisticated attacks, silently corrupt the computed position.

## Classification of interference
Interference affecting GNSS receivers can be classified along several dimensions. The first distinction is between **unintentional** and **intentional** interference.

### Unintentional interference
Unintentional interference arises from other radio systems whose signals happen to **overlap**, partially or totally, with the GNSS frequency bands. This can occur because a transmitter operates in a nearby frequency band and its signal bleeds into the GNSS band (out-of-band interference), or because harmonics and intermodulation products of a strong transmitter fall directly within the GNSS band (in-band interference). 

### Intentional interference
Intentional interference is deliberate and can target both civilian and military GNSS users. It is no longer limited to military or wartime scenarios: the growth of location-based services, GNSS-based road tolling, fleet management, pay-as-you-drive insurance, and safety-critical applications such as advanced driver assistance systems (ADAS) and emergency call (eCall) systems has made intentional interference a significant concern for commercial and civil security applications. Intentional interference can take two fundamentally different forms: it can simply aim to deny GNSS service (*jamming*), or it can attempt to manipulate the victim's computed position without detection (*spoofing* and related attacks).

A second classification dimension concerns the **spectral and temporal characteristics** of the interfering signal relative to the GNSS signal bandwidth $B_{GNSS}$. Interference is called **narrowband** when its bandwidth $B_i \ll B_{GNSS}$, and **wideband** when $B_i \geq B_{GNSS}$. A special case of narrowband interference is the **continuous wave** (*CW*), a pure sinusoidal tone whose bandwidth approaches zero. Interference can also be **pulsed**, characterized by a pulse width (PW), a pulse repetition frequency (PRF), and a duty cycle defined as $DC = PRF \times PW$, representing the fraction of time the interference is active.

Finally, intentional signal-level attacks on GNSS receivers are conventionally grouped into three categories: **[[GNSS jamming|jamming]]**, **meaconing**, and **[[GNSS spoofing|spoofing]]**. A fourth category, **cyberspoofing**, operates at the software or system level rather than at the RF signal level. Each of these is treated in detail in the following sections.
