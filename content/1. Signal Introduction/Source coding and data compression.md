In a digital communication system, the encoder at the transmitter performs two conceptually distinct operations before the signal is passed to the modulator: **source encoding** and **channel encoding**. On the receiver side, the decoder performs the corresponding inverse operations in reverse order.

## Encoding and decoding
The encoder accepts the raw digital output of a source, denoted $X$, and transforms it in two stages. 
1. Source encoding compresses $X$ into a more compact representation $Y = f(X)$, reducing the number of bits that must be transmitted. 
2. Channel encoding adds controlled redundancy to $Y$ to protect it against errors introduced by the channel. 

The decoder at the receiver performs these operations in reverse: channel decoding removes the redundancy and corrects errors, and source decoding applies the inverse mapping $f^{-1}(Y)$ to reconstruct an estimate $\hat{X}$ of the original source data. The interface between the user section and the channel section is entirely digital; the modulator converts the digital output of the encoder into an analogue waveform suitable for transmission, and the demodulator reverses this at the receiver.

## Source coding
Source coding, also known as **data compression**, is the process of representing information in a more compact form by removing redundancy. Real-world data sources, whether text, audio, images, or video, rarely produce perfectly random bit sequences. Instead, they exhibit statistical regularities: some symbols occur more frequently than others, consecutive samples are often correlated, and large regions of an image or long stretches of audio may be nearly identical. Source coding exploits these patterns to reduce the average number of bits per unit of information.

The primary goal is to eliminate or minimise redundancy. A simple illustration is variable-length coding, where shorter codewords are assigned to more frequent symbols and longer codewords to less frequent ones. Consider a source alphabet ${A, B, C, D}$ with frequencies 6, 3, 2, and 1 respectively over a block of 12 symbols. A fixed-length code requires 2 bits per symbol (since $\lceil \log_2 4 \rceil = 2$), giving 24 bits for the entire block. A variable-length code that assigns $A \to 0$, $B \to 10$, $C \to 110$, $D \to 111$ requires only 20 bits for the same block, a reduction of roughly 17%. The compression gain increases as the symbol frequency distribution becomes more skewed.

### Lossless and lossy compression
Source coding schemes are classified into two broad categories according to the fidelity requirement of the application.

- **Lossless compression** ensures that the reconstructed data $\hat{X}$ is identical to the original $X$. No information is discarded; the compression is achieved purely by exploiting statistical redundancy. Lossless compression is mandatory for any application where exact reconstruction is required, such as text files, executable programs, scientific measurements, telemetry data, IP packets, and digitised signals. Common lossless formats include ZIP for general data and PNG for images.

- **Lossy compression** sacrifices some degree of data accuracy in exchange for substantially higher compression ratios. The reconstructed output $\hat{X}$ is only an approximation of $X$, satisfying $\hat{X} \approx X$ rather than $\hat{X} = X$. The acceptable level of distortion is determined by the fidelity requirements of the application and, crucially, by the perceptual limitations of the end user or downstream system. Lossy compression is used extensively in multimedia: JPEG for still images, MP3 and AAC for audio, and the MPEG family of standards for video. In these domains, the human auditory and visual systems are insensitive to certain types of distortion, so information that would not be perceived anyway can be discarded without meaningful degradation of the user experience.

There is no universal compression format. The choice between lossless and lossy compression, and the specific algorithm used, depends on the nature of the source data, the fidelity requirements of the application, and the available computational resources. More sophisticated compression algorithms generally achieve higher compression ratios but require greater processing power, introducing a trade-off between compression efficiency and computational complexity.

### Video compression and differential encoding
Video compression illustrates the full power of source coding by exploiting two types of redundancy simultaneously. The first is spatial redundancy within each individual frame, which is compressed using the same techniques as still image compression. The second is temporal redundancy across successive frames: in most video sequences, consecutive frames are highly similar, and encoding only the differences between frames requires far fewer bits than encoding each frame independently.

This principle is implemented in the MPEG family of standards through a group of pictures (GOP) structure that distinguishes three frame types. An intraframe (I-frame) is encoded independently as a complete still image and serves as a random access point. A predicted frame (P-frame) is encoded as a set of differences relative to the most recent preceding I-frame or P-frame. A bi-directionally predicted frame (B-frame) is encoded with reference to both the preceding and the following reference frames, achieving the highest compression ratio of the three types at the cost of requiring future frames before it can be decoded.

The evolution of video coding standards from the early 1990s onward reflects a sustained effort to improve compression efficiency. MP3 established perceptual audio coding in the early 1990s. MPEG-2 Video, used in digital television and DVD, was followed by MPEG-4 Video and subsequently by the Advanced Video Coding standard (AVC, also known as H.264), which roughly halved the bit rate of MPEG-2 for equivalent quality. The High Efficiency Video Coding standard (HEVC, also known as H.265) achieved a further factor-of-two improvement and became the basis for HD and UHD distribution. Alongside these core codecs, delivery mechanisms such as DASH (Dynamic Adaptive Streaming over HTTP) and MMT have standardised how compressed media is packaged and streamed over networks.
